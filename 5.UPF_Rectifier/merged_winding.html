5.UPF_Rectifier · single-stage converter · magnetic merging

Can L2 and the LLC primary be one winding?

Yes, but only if the resonant tank moves to the secondary side. This page tests two ways of removing W2 with a time-domain simulation of the full single-stage converter (Dornbirn rectifier + half-bridge LLC on S1–S2, 230 Vac → 48 V / 60 W). Variant A keeps the tank on the primary and puts W1 on the LLC transformer. It works, but the current that W2 used to carry does not go away: it moves into Cr and Lr. Variant C moves Lr–Cr to the secondary. Then the primary sees exactly the voltage L2 saw, Lm takes the place of L2, and every Dornbirn formula stays exact.

1Variant A: W1 as a third winding, tank unchanged

In the baseline, W1 is coupled to W2, and W2 sits directly across the half bridge (v2 = vsw = ±VC). In variant A, W1 sees the transformer's magnetizing voltage vm. That voltage is a clean ±N·Vo only while the output diodes conduct:

di1/dt = (v_in − V_x − nk·v_m) / Lσ1 (was: … − nk·v_sw) i_m = i_r + nk·i1 − i_s' KCL at the magnetizing node (primary-referred) v_m = ±N·V_o output diodes conducting v_m = [(v_sw − v_Cr)/Lr + nk·(v_in − V_x)/Lσ1] / (1/Lr + nk²/Lσ1 + 1/Lm) output diodes off ⟨i_m⟩ ≈ nk·I_in(t) Cr blocks DC in i_r → 50 Hz flux bias in the transformer
Correcting my earlier answer. I said the circulating current i2 would disappear in this arrangement. The simulation shows it does not. The power that W2 used to move between the half bridge and the input loop still has to flow, and in variant A its only path is through Cr–Lr. The tank RMS current rises from 0.40 A to 1.28 A, and the Cr peak voltage from 84 V to 288 V.

2Why: the pump power through W1

The Dornbirn mechanism works by pushing power through W1 every switching period. In the simulation this is about : the half bridge sends it through the magnetic coupling into the input loop, and it returns to the bus through the diode bridge. This is the reason the converter draws a sinusoidal current, and it cannot be removed:

P_W1 = ⟨ nk · v2 · i1 ⟩ v2 = the voltage W1 is coupled to baseline: source of P_W1 = half bridge → W2 directly (no resonant element in the path) variant A: source of P_W1 = half bridge → Cr → Lr → transformer (the tank carries it) variant C: source of P_W1 = half bridge → primary directly (same as baseline, W2 = primary)

So the rule for merging is: the winding W1 is coupled to must sit directly across the half bridge, with no resonant element in series. The resonant tank has to be on the other side of the magnetizing branch, which means the secondary side.

3Variant C: move Lr–Cr to the secondary — the solution

primary voltage = v_sw = ±V_C exactly → W1 sees the same drive as with a separate W2 L2 → Lm, W2 → Np, nk = N_W1 / Np → R_in = 8·fs·Lσ1/nk, I_in = nk·T·v_in/(8·Lσ1) (unchanged, exact) λ_core,pk = nk·Lm·I_in,pk + V_C/(4·fs) → one magnetizing flux instead of two (coupled inductor + transformer) Lr′ = Lr/N² , Cr′ = N²·Cr → on the 48 V side

What changes at light load. With Lm in front of the tank, the output stage is a series resonant converter (SRC) driven by ±VC. Lm no longer takes part in the resonance, so the gain cannot rise above 1 and the bus does not have to climb as high to keep 48 V. In the simulation the bus is 438 V at 12 W, against 482 V for the baseline LLC. The switching frequency still follows fs ∝ 1/P, because the input side is unchanged.

Leakage placement. A two-winding transformer can always be written exactly as Lm on the primary terminals followed by all of its leakage on the secondary side (the Γ model). So Lr′ can be the primary–secondary leakage, or a small inductor on the 48 V side. What matters is that the W1–primary coupling keeps its designed leakage Lσ1 (a shunt or a separate choke, as in the integrated-magnetics page), and that no inductance sits in series with the primary itself.

5Full load, side by side

Variant C keeps every input-side number of the baseline: the same fs, the same bus and the same THD. Its switch current is 10 % lower, because the reactive current and the load current now flow through one winding and partly cancel. Variant A pays for removing W2 with a much larger tank current, three times the Cr voltage and a larger transformer flux. It also loses some THD, because its W1 drive voltage is not an exact square wave.

8How the simulation works, and its limits

  • Circuit model. Ideal switches with 50 % duty and no dead time. Ideal diodes in the input bridge and the output rectifier. Stiff bus capacitors (VC each) and a stiff 48 V output. Small resistances for damping: 0.5 Ω in the tank and 1 Ω in the W1 loop. Euler integration with 1000 steps per period, run to periodic steady state.
  • Quasi-static over the line. Each line angle (18 per quarter cycle) is solved as its own steady state. The line current is the period average of i1, and THD and PF come from those averages. The capacitor midpoint is modeled by forcing the period average of the current into it to zero. That is what puts the bias nk·Iin into the magnetizing current.
  • Operating point. Two conditions are solved together. First, bus power balance over the line cycle: what the diode bridge delivers equals what the half bridge takes. Second, Po = target. fs is found by a bracketed root search, and VC by bisection inside it.
  • Same parts everywhere. All variants use the single-stage design: nk = 4, Lσ1 = , Lr = , Cr = , N = . Lm is 1.86 mH (LLC, Ln = 8) in the baseline and variant A, and L2 = 1.17 mH in variant C. Variant C's tank on the secondary is the same Lr–Cr referred through N².
  • Not modeled. Core and switching losses, dead time and the Coss transitions, winding capacitance, and the three-winding leakage structure in detail. As on the other pages, FEM and a prototype are the next step. The model's energy balance closes within about 3 %.

9Verdict

Use variant C. Put W1 and the LLC primary on one core, with the primary connected directly across the half bridge so that its magnetizing inductance is L2. Move Lr–Cr to the 48 V side. The result is one magnetic core instead of two, the exact Dornbirn input behavior (THD 0.12 %, unity PF), 10 % lower switch RMS current, a lower light-load bus (438 V vs 482 V at 12 W) and a resonant capacitor that sees only about 18 V. Lm remains a free choice for the flux/ZVS trade-off.

For a journal paper, the claim is concrete: "a single-stage UPF converter with one magnetic component, whose input behavior is independent of the magnetizing inductance". The supporting evidence is the analytic L2-independence result, the pump-power argument for why the tank must sit on the secondary, and these simulations. Variant A is worth one paragraph as the obvious alternative and why it fails.
