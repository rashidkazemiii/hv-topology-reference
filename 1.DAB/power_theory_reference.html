Section 01
Instantaneous Power
The starting point — power at any instant is simply voltage times current.
Definition
p(t) = v(t) · i(t)

In AC circuits, voltage and current are sinusoids with amplitude and phase:

v(t) = Vp · cos(ωt + θ_v) i(t) = Ip · cos(ωt + θ_i)
Variables
SymbolMeaning
Vp, IpPeak (amplitude) values of voltage and current
θ_v, θ_iPhase angles of voltage and current
ωAngular frequency = 2π·f [rad/s]
tTime [s]
Key point

p(t) oscillates continuously — it is positive when energy flows into the load and negative when energy returns to the source. Average power is what we actually care about — how much net energy flows per second.

Section 02
The Key Trig Identity
Multiplying v(t)·i(t) splits instantaneous power into two terms — one constant and one oscillating.
Identity
cos(A) · cos(B) = ½·cos(A−B) + ½·cos(A+B)
Applying to p(t)
p(t) = Vp·Ip · cos(ωt+θ_v) · cos(ωt+θ_i) = ½·Vp·Ip·cos(θ_v−θ_i) + ½·Vp·Ip·cos(2ωt+θ_v+θ_i) ↑ ↑ CONSTANT — no time dependence OSCILLATES at 2ω → becomes Active Power P → averages to ZERO
Where does the ½ come from?

The ½ is not a convention — it is a direct mathematical result of the cos·cos identity. It cannot be removed or changed. This is also why RMS values are defined as Vp/√2 — to absorb this ½ so that the S = V·I* formula works without the ½ factor.

Why the 2ω term vanishes

cos(2ωt + constant) is a sinusoid — its average over any full cycle is exactly zero. Positive and negative halves cancel perfectly. Only the DC term ½·Vp·Ip·cos(θ_v−θ_i) survives averaging.

Section 03
Active Power P
The time-averaged power — the only part that does real work.
Formula
P = ½ · Vp · Ip · cos(θ_v − θ_i) [Watts, W]
How the angle controls P
Phase difference (θ_v − θ_i)cos valueResult
0°cos(0°) = 1Maximum P — voltage and current perfectly in phase
45°cos(45°) = 0.70770.7% of maximum — DAB slide operating point
90°cos(90°) = 0Zero active power — purely reactive
180°cos(180°) = −1Maximum negative P — power flows in reverse
Physical meaning

Active power P is what your electricity meter measures. It is what charges batteries, runs motors, heats resistors. It is the net energy transferred per second from source to load. P can be positive (forward), negative (reverse), or zero.

Section 04
Why Complex Numbers — Phasors
Time-domain calculations with cos and sin are messy. Phasors represent sinusoids as complex numbers — making multiplication, division, and angle tracking trivial.
Euler's Formula — the bridge
e^(iθ) = cos(θ) + i·sin(θ) Therefore: cos(θ) = Re[e^(iθ)] ← real part sin(θ) = Im[e^(iθ)] ← imaginary part
Converting a sinusoid to a phasor
Time domain: v(t) = Vp · cos(ωt + θ_v) Phasor form: Ṽ = Vp · e^(iθ_v) The e^(iωt) factor is dropped — it is understood to be there. We only track amplitude Vp and phase angle θ_v.
Why this helps
1
Multiplication becomes addition of angles
Instead of expanding cos(A)·cos(B) with trig identities, multiplying phasors just adds exponents: e^(iα) · e^(iβ) = e^(i(α+β))
2
Division becomes subtraction of angles
Dividing phasors: e^(iα) / e^(iβ) = e^(i(α−β)). This is exactly how impedance (Z = V/I) works in the frequency domain.
3
Impedance of an inductor becomes simple
From V = L·di/dt — if i = I·e^(iωt) then di/dt = iω·I·e^(iωt), so Z_L = V/I = iωL. A single complex number replaces differential equations.
Section 05
Complex Power S = ½·Ṽ·Ĩ*
The complete derivation of complex power from phasor definitions.
Define phasors
Ṽ = Vp · e^(iθ_v) ← voltage phasor (peak) Ĩ = Ip · e^(iθ_i) ← current phasor (peak) Ĩ* = Ip · e^(−iθ_i) ← complex conjugate: flip sign of angle
Compute ½·Ṽ·Ĩ*
½·Ṽ·Ĩ* = ½ · Vp·e^(iθ_v) · Ip·e^(−iθ_i) = ½ · Vp·Ip · e^(i(θ_v − θ_i)) ← angles subtract = ½ · Vp·Ip · [cos(θ_v−θ_i) + i·sin(θ_v−θ_i)] ↑ ↑ Real part = P Imaginary part = Q
Result
S = ½·Ṽ·Ĩ* = P + jQ [Volt-Ampere, VA] P = Re[S] = ½·Vp·Ip·cos(θ_v−θ_i) ← Active power [W] Q = Im[S] = ½·Vp·Ip·sin(θ_v−θ_i) ← Reactive power [VAR]
Connection to section 02

The result Re[S] = ½·Vp·Ip·cos(θ_v−θ_i) is exactly the same P we derived from the trig identity in Section 02. The complex power formula is just a more elegant way to arrive at the same answer — it packages P and Q together in one calculation.

Section 06
Why Conjugate I* and Not I?
This is the most commonly misunderstood step. The conjugate is not arbitrary — it is essential for angle subtraction.
What happens without conjugate — WRONG
½·Ṽ·Ĩ = ½ · Vp·e^(iθ_v) · Ip·e^(+iθ_i) = ½·Vp·Ip · e^(i(θ_v + θ_i)) ← angles ADD — WRONG Re[...] = ½·Vp·Ip·cos(θ_v + θ_i) ← not the power angle!
What happens with conjugate — CORRECT
½·Ṽ·Ĩ* = ½ · Vp·e^(iθ_v) · Ip·e^(−iθ_i) = ½·Vp·Ip · e^(i(θ_v − θ_i)) ← angles SUBTRACT — CORRECT Re[...] = ½·Vp·Ip·cos(θ_v − θ_i) ← true phase difference = power angle ✓
Why subtraction is what we need

Power depends on the difference in phase between voltage and current — how much they are out of step with each other. If θ_v = θ_i (same phase), P is maximum. The conjugate flips the sign of θ_i so that when we multiply, the exponents subtract instead of add — giving us the angle difference that determines real power.

RMS convention — no ½ needed

If you use RMS values (Vrms = Vp/√2, Irms = Ip/√2) then S = Vrms·Irms* with no ½ factor. The ½ is already absorbed into the RMS definition. Both conventions give the same result.

Section 07
P, Q, S and PF — Defined
The four power quantities every power electronics engineer must know.
P — Active Power [W]
P = Re[S] = ½·Vp·Ip·cos(θ_v−θ_i)
Does real work. Charges batteries, runs motors, heats resistors. What your electricity meter measures. Can be positive or negative.
Q — Reactive Power [VAR]
Q = Im[S] = ½·Vp·Ip·sin(θ_v−θ_i)
Oscillates between source and inductor/capacitor. Does no net work but causes circulating current and conduction losses.
S — Apparent Power [VA]
|S| = √(P² + Q²) = ½·Vp·Ip
Total burden on the source — what sizes your cables, switches, and transformer. Always ≥ P.
PF — Power Factor
PF = P / |S| = cos(θ_v − θ_i)
Ratio of useful to total power. PF=1 is ideal. In DAB, PF drops when V₁/n ≠ V₂ because Q grows relative to P.
In the DAB specifically

Section 08
Sine vs Cosine — When Each Appears and Why
This is the source of most confusion. The roles of sin and cos appear to swap in the DAB — here is the complete explanation.
cosine → Real part → P (general AC)
Re[e^(iφ)] = cos(φ)
cos(0°)=1 → max power when in phase
cos(90°)=0 → zero power when 90° apart
General formula: P = ½·Vp·Ip·cos(θ_v−θ_i)
sine → Imaginary part → Q (general AC)
Im[e^(iφ)] = sin(φ)
sin(0°)=0 → no reactive power when in phase
sin(90°)=1 → max reactive power at 90°
General formula: Q = ½·Vp·Ip·sin(θ_v−θ_i)
Why DAB seems backwards — the 90° rotation from 1/(iωL)

In a general resistive circuit, cos→P and sin→Q. But the DAB has a purely inductive impedance: Z = iωL. Dividing by i is the same as multiplying by −i, which rotates everything by −90°. This rotation swaps the roles of sin and cos in the final result:

General circuit (resistive reference): P uses cos(φ), Q uses sin(φ) DAB circuit — divide by iωL rotates by −90°: IL = (V₁ − V₂·e^(iφ)) / (i·ω·L) ↑ this 1/i = −i rotates by −90° Result after rotation: P = V₁·V₂·sin(φ) / (ω·L) ← sin came from imaginary, now in real part Q = (V₁²−V₁·V₂·cos(φ)) / (ω·L) ← cos came from real, now in imaginary part
Complete summary table
ContextP formulaQ formulaReason

DAB (inductive Z = iωL)V₁·V₂·sin(φ)/(ωL)(V₁²−V₁·V₂·cos(φ))/(ωL)90° rotation from 1/(iωL) swaps sin and cos
Section 09
Full DAB Derivation — Step by Step
From the circuit phasor model to the final P and Q formulas, every algebraic step shown.
Setup — FHA phasor model
Two H-bridges → two sinusoidal voltage sources: Ṽ₁ = V₁ ← reference, angle = 0 Ṽ₂ = V₂·e^(iφ) ← lags by φ in this convention Inductor impedance: Z_L = i·ω·L
1
Current phasor — Ohm's law
ĨL = (Ṽ₁ − Ṽ₂) / Z_L = (V₁ − V₂·e^(iφ)) / (i·ω·L)
2
Take the complex conjugate ĨL*
ĨL* = (V₁ − V₂·e^(−iφ)) / (−i·ω·L) Replace 1/(−i) = i: ĨL* = i·(V₁ − V₂·e^(−iφ)) / (ω·L)
3
Expand e^(−iφ) using Euler
e^(−iφ) = cos(φ) − i·sin(φ) V₁ − V₂·e^(−iφ) = (V₁ − V₂·cosφ) + i·V₂·sinφ ↑ real part ↑ imaginary part
4
Multiply by i — this causes the sin/cos swap
i · [(V₁−V₂cosφ) + i·V₂sinφ] = i·(V₁−V₂cosφ) + i²·V₂sinφ = i·(V₁−V₂cosφ) − V₂sinφ (i² = −1) Collecting: Real part: −V₂·sin(φ) Imaginary part: (V₁ − V₂·cos(φ))
5
Multiply by V₁/(ωL) to get S = ½·Ṽ₁·ĨL*
S = (V₁/ωL) · [−V₂sinφ + i·(V₁−V₂cosφ)] Separating (and flipping sign convention for φ as lag): P = Re[S] = V₁·V₂·sin(φ) / (ω·L) ✓ Q = Im[S] = (V₁² − V₁·V₂·cos(φ)) / (ω·L) ✓
Section 10
Why Average Over Half Cycle?
The integration limits in the slide run from 0 to 1/(2Fsw), multiplied by 2Fsw. Two independent reasons justify this.
The formula from the slide
P = 2·Fsw · ∫₀^(1/2Fsw) p(t) dt = (1 / half-period) · ∫ p(t) dt ← standard time-average formula
R1
Reason 1 — Half-wave symmetry
DAB waveforms are perfectly half-wave symmetric. The negative half cycle is an exact mirror of the positive half cycle — same shape, opposite sign. Therefore the average over one half cycle equals the average over a full cycle. Integrating over half a period and multiplying by 2Fsw gives the same result as integrating over a full period and multiplying by Fsw. It is a valid mathematical shortcut.
R2
Reason 2 — The switching sequence repeats every half period
In the DAB, one complete energy transfer event happens in T/2. Q1/Q4 conduct during the first half cycle — energy flows forward. Q2/Q3 conduct in the second half — exact mirror image. The half period already contains all the information about power transfer. There is nothing new in the second half that the first does not already tell you.
The 2·Fsw factor explained

2·Fsw = 1/(T/2) = the reciprocal of the integration interval. This is exactly the time-average operator: average = (1/interval) × integral over that interval. So the formula is simply computing a time average — the half-cycle is just the chosen interval, justified by symmetry.

Section 11
Worked Example — Slide Values
V₁=100V, V₂=150V, L=10µH, f=100kHz, φ=45°. Verifying all four power quantities.
Step 1 — Compute ω·L
ω = 2π × f = 2π × 100,000 = 628,318 rad/s ω·L = 628,318 × 10×10⁻⁶ = 6.283 Ω
Step 2 — Results
Calculated power quantities
P (active power) 100×150×sin(45°) / 6.283 = 1,688 W ✓
Q (reactive power) (100² − 100×150×cos45°) / 6.283 = −96.6 VAR
|S| (apparent power) √(1688² + 96.6²) = 1,691 VA
PF (power factor) 1688 / 1691 = 0.998 (excellent)
Why Q is negative? V₂ > V₁ — secondary supplies reactive power back to primary
Why PF is so high at φ=45°

At φ=45° with these values, Q is very small (−96.6 VAR) compared to P (1,688 W). This means almost all the current through the inductor is doing useful work. The DAB operates efficiently here because the voltage ratio V₂/V₁ = 1.5 partially compensates — the secondary's higher voltage contributes reactive power back, nearly cancelling the primary's reactive demand.

The fundamental DAB design goal

Maximize the P/Q ratio — maximize useful power relative to circulating reactive current. This is achieved by: (1) operating at matched voltage ratio V₁/n ≈ V₂, (2) choosing L small enough that rated power is reached at moderate φ (<60°), and (3) using advanced modulation (EPS, TPS) at light load to reduce reactive current when φ is small.
