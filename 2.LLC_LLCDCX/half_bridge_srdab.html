Half-bridge resonant converter, primary and secondary, step by step

Two half bridges, one on each side of a transformer, with Lr and Cr in between. Every switch runs at 50 % duty with a short dead time. The secondary is delayed by a phase shift θ, and θ decides how much power flows. Press play and follow the gate signals.

1Waveforms: primary on the left, secondary on the right

Reading the schematic at the top. The moving dashes show the current path and direction at the selected moment: blue on the primary, green on the secondary, purple through Coss during a dead time. Each switch is drawn as channel, body diode and Coss. When the top switch conducts, the loop closes through Cbus (AC part) and Vbus (DC part). When the bottom switch conducts, the loop is short: tank → bottom switch → back. On the secondary, the DC part goes to R and the ripple to Cout.

Why does the primary current lag vA? Above resonance the tank Lr–Cr is an inductor overall. An inductor's current lags its voltage by 90°, and the voltage across the tank is the difference between the two square waves. Put together, the current crosses zero φ after vA rises:

How a half bridge makes a square wave. Node A switches between 0 V (Q2 on) and Vbus = 400 V (Q1 on). Cr is in series and blocks the DC part: it charges to about Vbus/2 = 200 V and keeps that voltage. What is left for Lr and the transformer is a ±200 V square wave. The secondary works the same way: node B switches between 0 and Vout, and Cdc holds Vout/2 so the transformer sees no DC. The current in Lr depends on the difference between the two square waves.

2How the secondary switches turn on and off

1. The secondary is a second square-wave generator. Q3 and Q4 get the same 50 % gate signals as Q1 and Q2, only delayed by θ. Q3 on → node B = Vout. Q4 on → node B = 0 V. The controller decides when they switch, not the current.

2. What a plain diode rectifier would do. If Q3 and Q4 were only diodes, node B would follow the current. Current flowing into B → D3 conducts → node B = Vout. Current flowing out of B → D4 → node B = 0. A diode bridge switches exactly when the current crosses zero (lower bar in the secondary chart, right).

3. What the active bridge does instead. It switches θ after the primary. The current already crossed zero at φ, which is a little earlier. In between (red), the old switch Q4 is still on, but the current now flows drain → source through it, which a diode could never do. Energy goes back from the output: this is the backflow. Then Q4 turns off and the dead time starts. The current, already in the new direction, swings node B up (purple), D3 picks it up (amber), and Q3 turns on at zero voltage. For the rest of the half period, Q3 carries the current source → drain like a diode without the 0.7 V drop (green): a synchronous rectifier.

4. The one rule. A secondary switch gets ZVS if the current has already reversed when its partner turns off. So the gate edge θ must come after the zero crossing φ:

primary ZVS: φ > 0 (current still negative when Q1 turns on) secondary ZVS: φ < θ (current already positive when Q4 turns off) both: 0 < φ < θ backflow time: (θ − φ)/360° · T

3Basics you need first

An inductor's current cannot jump

v = L·di/dt. The voltage across Lr only sets the slope of its current. When a switch opens, Lr keeps its current flowing and forces it into whatever path is left: a Coss, then a body diode. All of ZVS comes from this.

A capacitor's voltage cannot jump

i = C·dv/dt. To move a node by ΔV you must move a charge Q = C·ΔV. A current I does that in a time t = C·ΔV/I. That is the swing time in the dead time.

A MOSFET has three current paths

1) The channel: with the gate on it conducts in both directions, like a small resistor. 2) The body diode: always there, conducts only source → drain, about 0.7 V drop. 3) The output capacitance Coss: when the switch is off and its voltage changes, current flows into or out of it. The schematic draws all three.

ZVS

Zero-voltage switching: the MOSFET turns on when its drain–source voltage is already zero, because the current has already emptied its Coss and its body diode conducts. Turning on at a voltage V instead dumps ½·C·V² into the channel, every switching event.

Dead time

The two switches of one leg must never be on together, or they short the bus. So between "one off" and "the other on" there is a short time with both off. During that time the tank current swings the node voltage across the leg.

Why Cr and Cdc hold a DC voltage

Node A switches between 0 and 400 V, so it has a 200 V average. A transformer cannot take DC, so the series capacitor Cr takes it: about 200 V, constant. What is left for Lr and the transformer is a ±200 V square wave. Cdc does the same on the secondary, with Vout/2.

5Zoom: what happens inside the dead time

The rule for ZVS, in one sentence: when a switch turns off, the current must still be flowing in the direction that swings the node toward the other rail, and it must be large enough to finish the swing within the dead time.

swing time ≈ 2·C_oss · V / |i| must be < dead time primary (Q1): i_res < 0 when Q2 turns off secondary (Q3): i_res > 0 when Q4 turns off

Try it. Lower θ to about 10°: the current at the switching edges gets small, the swing becomes slow, and the switch turns on before the node arrives (red). Set R to 15 Ω (light load): Vout rises, M becomes larger than 1, and the primary loses ZVS first. Set R to 4 Ω (heavy load): Vout drops, M is below 1, and the secondary loses it first. A dead time that is too long is not free either. After the swing, the body diode conducts for the rest of the dead time, and that costs conduction loss.

6Why can't θ be zero?

It can: move the θ slider to 0° and look at the result. Two things go wrong.

  • No power. The power is P = 2M·V_bus²·sin θ/(π²·X). At θ = 0 the two square waves are in phase, so sin θ = 0 and nothing is transferred. With a resistor as load, the output capacitor then discharges and Vout falls to zero.
  • No ZVS. The dead-time swing is driven by the current at the switching edge. With almost no current, the node cannot swing, and all four switches turn on with voltage across them: hard switching (red circles). Some current still flows, but it only circulates (reactive) and does not deliver power.

So θ is the power knob, and with a resistive load it sets the output voltage. Increasing θ from 0 toward 90° increases the power and the ZVS current. With a battery instead of a resistor, a negative θ would reverse the power and charge the bus from the battery: the reverse mode in the app note. A resistor cannot give energy back, so here θ stays between 0° and 90°.

Why Vout changes with R. For a fixed θ this converter behaves like a current source: the average output current is set by θ and the tank, not by the load. So Vout ≈ I·R. A lighter load (larger R) gives a higher voltage. A real controller closes the loop and adjusts θ to hold 48 V. The θ for 48 V button does that once.

7How to choose θ (TI app note SDAA372)

The app note's goal: pick θ from the voltage ratio M = V_out/(n·V_bus) so that all four switches keep ZVS, and set the power with the switching frequency. With the first-harmonic approximation, both sides have ZVS when

θ > θ_boundary = arccos[ min(M, 1/M) ] (correct in the app note)

What I found when checking the app note:

  • Eq. 6 is wrong. It forgets that an inductive tank makes the current lag by 90°, the "j" in jX. The correct current is i ∝ M·sinθ·sin ωt − (1 − M·cosθ)·cos ωt. The later equations 11 and 13 already use this correct form.
  • Eq. 14–15 are curve fits, not solutions. Eq. 13 has no answer below 90°.
  • Eq. 15 drops below the ZVS boundary for M > 1.6. There the primary switches hard. The cause is the extra factor (1 − 0.25·|M − 1|).

Simple fix: remove that factor.

θ = arccos[ 0.8 · min(M, 1/M) ]

8Numbers used

The simulation is a time-domain model of the real circuit. It uses ideal switches with body diodes, a constant Coss on each switch, the dead times, and a 2 Ω tank resistance (windings, Rds(on), ESR). Cbus, Cout and Cdc are large, so their voltages are constant within a period. Vout is found by iteration until the power delivered equals Vout²/R. The simulation starts from the first-harmonic solution and runs until the waveforms stop changing. The transformer magnetizing inductance is left out to keep it simple.
